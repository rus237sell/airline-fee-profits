# Airline fee profits

**Goal:** Test how much of U.S. airline operating profit is really the two fees BTS reports separately (baggage fees and reservation change fees), and what profit looks like without them.

**Macro steps:**
1. Pulled BTS annual results for all U.S. scheduled passenger airlines (2019 to 2025, continuous): operating revenue, operating profit, net income, baggage fees, change fees.
2. Ran a counterfactual: operating profit minus reported fees, expenses held flat. That is an upper bound on the fees' contribution, since checked bags do cost something to handle and BTS does not split that cost out.
3. Pulled Big 4 (Delta, United, American, Southwest) revenue and operating income live from SEC EDGAR 10-K facts for margin context.
4. Wrote the industry table to `results.csv`, the Big 4 table to `big4_margins.csv`, and charted reported vs ex-fee operating profit (`results.png`).
5. Split the fee mix (`fee_mix.csv`, `fee_mix.png`): baggage vs change fees separately, indexed to 2019, because they moved in opposite directions after 2019.
6. Stress-tested the flat-expense counterfactual with a handling-cost sensitivity (`sensitivity.csv`, `sensitivity.png`): baggage handling assumed to cost 0 / 25 / 50 / 75% of baggage fees, so net fee contribution is fees minus the cost that would vanish with them. Added `test_fee_profits.py`.
7. Cumulated the series and grouped it into eras (`cumulative.csv`, `cumulative.png`, `eras.csv`): 2019, the 2020-2021 loss years, 2022, and the 2023-2025 recovery, so period totals are visible next to the single-year shares. Tests now 22.
8. Divided every total by that year's reporting-carrier count (`per_airline.csv`, `per_airline.png`): the count rose from 21 in 2019 to 25 in 2025, so totals mix a scale change with a performance change. Tests now 29.
9. Joined the Big 4 EDGAR totals to the BTS industry totals (`big4_vs_rest.csv`, `big4_vs_rest.png`): the rest of the industry is the implied residual (industry minus Big 4, approximate because the sources define revenue slightly differently). Tests now 36.

**Versions:** v1, initial analysis. v2: filled the 2021-2022 gap so the BTS series runs continuously 2019 to 2025 (2021 loss year, 2022 first profit year back); trend chart now covers all profit years. v3: fee-mix cut -- baggage vs change fees split out with a stacked chart and 2019 index. v4: handling-cost sensitivity (net fees and adjusted ex-fee profit at four assumed baggage-handling cost rates, plus the cost rate at which net fees fall to half of operating profit) and the first test suite. v5: cumulative / era lens -- cumulative totals from 2019 and era totals (loss years, first profit year, recovery), with a cumulative chart; 7 new tests (22 total). v6: per-airline lens -- every total divided by that year's reporting-carrier count, with a per-airline chart and 2019 comparisons for profit and fees per airline; 7 new tests (29 total). v7: Big 4 vs rest-of-industry split -- EDGAR Big 4 revenue and operating profit aggregated and subtracted from the BTS industry totals, with a split chart; 7 new tests (36 total).

**Short results:** Reported fees are only about 3.4% of revenue, but they equal 62% of operating profit in 2023, 62% in 2024, and 75% in 2025. Without them (expenses held flat), industry operating profit falls from $11.4B to $2.9B in 2025 and the operating margin thins from 4.5% to 1.1%. The new 2022 row is the extreme case: fees of $7.8B were 98.7% of the $7.9B operating profit, leaving ex-fee operating profit of just $0.1B. In each of 2023 to 2025, baggage plus change fees alone exceeded the industry's entire after-tax net income. In 2019 the same fees were 41% of a much larger $20.8B operating profit. The mix flipped underneath a flat total: total fees were $8.6B in 2019 and $8.5B in 2025, but baggage rose 27.6% ($5.8B to $7.4B, 67.4% to 87.1% of fees) while change fees fell 60.7% ($2.8B to $1.1B) and never recovered -- the fee story is now almost entirely baggage. The v4 sensitivity sizes the first offset: even if handling a bag cost half the fee collected, net fees were still 42% of 2025 operating profit ($4.8B, adjusted ex-fee profit $6.6B at a 2.6% margin, not $2.9B) and 56% in 2022; net fees fall to half of operating profit at a 38% handling cost in 2025, but only 23% in 2023 and 21% in 2024, so the majority-of-profit reading is robust for 2022 and 2025 and fragile for 2023-2024. The second offset runs the other way: the counterfactual understates total ancillary dependence, because seat selection, food, and other ancillary revenue are not in the BTS fee lines at all. Cumulated over 2019-2025, reported fees were $51.1B against just $3.0B of cumulative operating profit and -$0.9B of cumulative net income, so cumulative operating profit ex fees is -$48.1B -- the pandemic losses nearly erase the good years, and the fees are most of the bridge back to positive. The recovery alone is cleaner: in 2023-2025 fees were $25.0B, 65.6% of the $38.1B operating profit, leaving $13.1B ex fees. Per airline the recovery looks thinner: revenue per reporting airline in 2025 ($10.10B) is 8.1% above 2019 ($9.34B), so scale has recovered, but operating profit per airline ($0.46B) is 54% below 2019 ($0.99B) and ex-fee operating profit per airline fell from $0.58B to $0.12B. Fees per airline actually fell 17% ($0.41B to $0.34B) -- the fee share of profit rose because ex-fee profit per carrier collapsed, not because fees per carrier grew. Big 4 context: combined operating margin was 6.1% in 2025 (Delta 9.2%, United 8.0%, American 2.7%, Southwest 1.5%). Split against the industry, the Big 4 took about 81% of revenue in each of 2023-2025 but 98% of operating profit in 2023, 104% in 2024, and 109% in 2025 -- a share above 100% means the rest lost money: the other carriers combined earned $0.22B in 2023 (0.5% margin) and lost $0.53B in 2024 (-1.1%) and $1.03B in 2025 (-2.2%), so the industry profit the fees are compared against is entirely a Big 4 profit (rest is an implied residual across two sources, so its dollars are approximate; BTS does not split fees by carrier, so the rest's ex-fee result cannot be computed).
