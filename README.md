# Airline fee profits

**Goal:** Test how much of U.S. airline operating profit is really the two fees BTS reports separately (baggage fees and reservation change fees), and what profit looks like without them.

**Macro steps:**
1. Pulled BTS annual results for all U.S. scheduled passenger airlines (2019 to 2025, continuous): operating revenue, operating profit, net income, baggage fees, change fees.
2. Ran a counterfactual: operating profit minus reported fees, expenses held flat. That is an upper bound on the fees' contribution, since checked bags do cost something to handle and BTS does not split that cost out.
3. Pulled Big 4 (Delta, United, American, Southwest) revenue and operating income live from SEC EDGAR 10-K facts for margin context.
4. Wrote the industry table to `results.csv`, the Big 4 table to `big4_margins.csv`, and charted reported vs ex-fee operating profit (`results.png`).
5. Split the fee mix (`fee_mix.csv`, `fee_mix.png`): baggage vs change fees separately, indexed to 2019, because they moved in opposite directions after 2019.
6. Stress-tested the flat-expense counterfactual with a handling-cost sensitivity (`sensitivity.csv`, `sensitivity.png`): baggage handling assumed to cost 0 / 25 / 50 / 75% of baggage fees, so net fee contribution is fees minus the cost that would vanish with them. Added `test_fee_profits.py` (15 tests).

**Versions:** v1, initial analysis. v2: filled the 2021-2022 gap so the BTS series runs continuously 2019 to 2025 (2021 loss year, 2022 first profit year back); trend chart now covers all profit years. v3: fee-mix cut -- baggage vs change fees split out with a stacked chart and 2019 index. v4: handling-cost sensitivity (net fees and adjusted ex-fee profit at four assumed baggage-handling cost rates, plus the cost rate at which net fees fall to half of operating profit) and the first test suite.

**Short results:** Reported fees are only about 3.4% of revenue, but they equal 62% of operating profit in 2023, 62% in 2024, and 75% in 2025. Without them (expenses held flat), industry operating profit falls from $11.4B to $2.9B in 2025 and the operating margin thins from 4.5% to 1.1%. The new 2022 row is the extreme case: fees of $7.8B were 98.7% of the $7.9B operating profit, leaving ex-fee operating profit of just $0.1B. In each of 2023 to 2025, baggage plus change fees alone exceeded the industry's entire after-tax net income. In 2019 the same fees were 41% of a much larger $20.8B operating profit. The mix flipped underneath a flat total: total fees were $8.6B in 2019 and $8.5B in 2025, but baggage rose 27.6% ($5.8B to $7.4B, 67.4% to 87.1% of fees) while change fees fell 60.7% ($2.8B to $1.1B) and never recovered -- the fee story is now almost entirely baggage. The v4 sensitivity sizes the first offset: even if handling a bag cost half the fee collected, net fees were still 42% of 2025 operating profit ($4.8B, adjusted ex-fee profit $6.6B at a 2.6% margin, not $2.9B) and 56% in 2022; net fees fall to half of operating profit at a 38% handling cost in 2025, but only 23% in 2023 and 21% in 2024, so the majority-of-profit reading is robust for 2022 and 2025 and fragile for 2023-2024. The second offset runs the other way: the counterfactual understates total ancillary dependence, because seat selection, food, and other ancillary revenue are not in the BTS fee lines at all. Big 4 context: combined operating margin was 6.1% in 2025 (Delta 9.2%, United 8.0%, American 2.7%, Southwest 1.5%).
